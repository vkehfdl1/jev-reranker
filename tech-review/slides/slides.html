Introduction

Question, approach, and thesis

Research question

A decision model returns a typed answer with probabilities instead of generated text. Can such a model serve as the second-stage reranker of a retrieval pipeline — and at what quality, cost, and latency compared with dedicated cross-encoder rerankers?

What I did

  • Compared 7 systems under identical conditions: 100 candidates per query, one seeded shuffle, one prompt per mode, no tuning.
  • Two benchmarks with different label density: MIRACLReranking (2.2 positives/query) and JQaRAReranking (9.7 positives/query).
  • Measured nDCG@10 (primary), billed cost per 1,000 queries, sequential and 64-way latency; paired bootstrap (10,000 resamples) for every comparison.
  • Opened the black box: how Jev-style models are trained and called, and what their score distributions look like when used as rankers.
Thesis. “Does the decision model replace the reranker?” is the wrong question. The same Jev 1.14 loses to Cohere Rerank 4-pro on MIRACL (0.750 vs 0.793) and wins on JQaRA (0.885 vs 0.819). The ranking depends on the data and on how the model is invoked, so the choice has to be measured on one’s own data, with cost and latency in the loop.

Related review in the class library: “Tech Review: LLM Rerankers as a Second Stage over BM25” surveys LLM reranking methods; this review adds measurements and the mechanics of decision models.

All numbers: results_final/FINAL.md (K=100, one run per system and dataset).

Background

Two-stage retrieval and the role of the reranker

Why a second stage

  • The first stage scores query and document independently, so the index can be built offline and searched quickly — but it compares them only coarsely.
  • The reranker reads the query together with each of the K candidates. Per-query cost is therefore proportional to K, and final quality is bounded by first-stage recall.
  • BEIR reports that re-ranking and late-interaction models give the best zero-shot average, but at high computational cost; ColBERT attributes that cost to pushing every query–document pair through a large network.

What this implies for the comparison

  • The unit of cost is the candidate list (100 passages per query), not a single document.
  • Providers price it differently: per input token (decision models) or per search unit of up to 100 documents (Cohere) — so the cheaper option depends on passage length.
  • Reference points in our samples: BM25 order gives nDCG@10 0.443 on MIRACL; a random order gives 0.114 on JQaRA.
Khattab & Zaharia, ColBERT (arXiv 2004.12832); Thakur et al., BEIR (arXiv 2104.08663) — abstracts. Baselines: results_final/FINAL.md.

Background

Cross-encoder rerankers

  • Every query token can attend to every passage token, which is why cross-encoders are more accurate than independent encoders — and why they only run on the top-K.
  • Scores are pointwise: each candidate is scored on its own, so the result does not depend on candidate order.

Setwisefewer inferences than pairwiseBetween pairwise accuracy and pointwise costNeeds a sorting procedure over setsSetwise paper

What the literature reports

  • Instructed LLMs can match supervised rankers (RankGPT); pairwise prompting (PRP) makes moderate-sized open models competitive.
  • Pointwise is efficient but less effective; pairwise is effective but costly (Setwise paper).

Where decision models fit

  • Choice over N options = listwise / setwise selection with a probability for every option.
  • Noul = pointwise P(yes); many Noul questions can share one request.

How it works

Decision models (System One): what goes in, what comes out

Not an LLM that writes answers (vendor documentation; not independently verified)

  • Returns a typed answer with probabilities; the caller fixes the answer space. No generated text.
  • Trained with RLCD — reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions — rather than RLHF or RLVR. Calibration holds across many predictions, not for a single answer.
  • Questions in one request share a state that is read once and are evaluated in parallel.
  • Jev 1.14: $0.042 / M input tokens, output free; 262K context on OpenRouter.
  • Architecture, size and training data are not disclosed in the documents I read.
  • One API, 22 models from 13 makers — I switched Jev → openai/gpt-6-luna-decisions ($0.10 / M) by changing the model name.
My observation. gpt-6-luna reports output_tokens = 0: consistent with reading probabilities out of one forward pass, like Qwen3-Reranker’s yes/no logit. An inference, not documented.
TypeSafe docs: System One, AI primer, Models, Choice, Noul; OpenRouter Jev guide. Output-token observation: my own API calls, 2026-11-01.

How it works

Turning a decision model into a reranker: two invocation modes

Listwise: one Choice question over all 100 passages

  • Only ~6 candidates (Jev) or ~20 (luna) get non-zero mass; the rest tie at 0 and keep the presented order.

Batched pointwise: 100 Noul questions in one request

  • Every passage is scored, but at 0.01 resolution ~63% tie (Jev); input tokens +18% (Jev) / +99% (luna) vs. listwise.

Bars: one real JQaRA query (test_query831), 100 candidates in the order the model received them; dark = labelled positive. All systems: same seeded shuffle, sort by score, ties broken by presented position, one fixed prompt per mode.

Not strictly pointwise. TypeSafe’s own re-ranking cookbook sends one request per (query, candidate) pair; mine puts all 100 passages in one state, so every question can see the whole list. I did not compare the two forms.
Implementation: clients.py, jqara_run.py. Measurements: results_final/ANALYSIS.md (A); figure: tech-review/slides/make_figures.py.

Results

Price–performance and latency–performance: decision models vs. rerankers

What the frontiers show

  • Frontier (circled): Jev pointwise, Jev listwise and Cohere v3.5 in three of the four panels; v3.5 drops out of the JQaRA latency panel. Cohere 4-pro, 4-fast and gpt-6-luna are on no frontier.
  • JQaRA: Jev pointwise beats 4-pro by 0.066 nDCG@10 and is $1.36 / 1K queries cheaper and 0.42 s faster — 4-pro is significantly dominated.
  • MIRACL: 4-pro is tied with v3.5 at 2.5× the price. Against Jev pointwise: $0.37 saved per 0.01 nDCG@10 given up ($1.61 and 0.41 s saved for −0.044).
  • gpt-6-luna is significantly dominated in all four panels, in both modes.
  • Under load (64 in flight, JQaRA): Jev listwise slows to 2.78 s mean / 6.84 s p95; Cohere 4-pro stays at 1.05 s.
Cost model. Decision models: input tokens × price (Jev $0.042/M, luna $0.10/M). Cohere: flat $0.0025 per query (4-pro). Break-even ≈ 60K (Jev) / 25K (luna) tokens per query; our 100-passage queries use 11–36K.

  • Σ P(yes) is 22–24 per query while qrels list 2.2 / 9.7 positives. The prompt’s notion of relevance (“helps answer the question”) is broader than the labels, so this is no proof of mis-calibration — but P(yes) should be used as a ranking score only.

  • The gap between modes is negligible on MIRACL and large on JQaRA; at rank 1 the two modes are about equal.
  • Within one vendor, tiers differ by dataset: Cohere 4-pro 0.819 vs. 4-fast 0.686 and v3.5 0.701 on JQaRA, while on MIRACL 4-pro and v3.5 are tied (0.793, 0.795) and 4-fast is 0.752.

Confounded — not separated by this design

  • Label density: 2.2 vs. 9.7 positives per query. Shallow listwise support costs more when there are more positives to rank.
  • Input truncation: MIRACL passages were cut to 218–533 characters, JQaRA passages were not.
  • Label semantics: JQaRA marks a passage positive when it contains the answer string (plus filtering); MIRACL uses graded human relevance judgments.
  • Language and domain: 18 languages of Wikipedia queries vs. Japanese quiz questions.

Hypotheses (untested)

  • Quiz questions are answerable from world knowledge, which may help a model judge whether a passage contains the answer.
  • Vendor documentation says Jev is weaker outside English, yet it is first on Japanese JQaRA — unexplained.
  • Possible contamination of any model with JAQKET/Wikipedia material cannot be excluded.

Limitations

Limitations and negative results

Threats to validity

  • One run per system and dataset. Re-running MIRACL changed scores by −0.006 to +0.002 (e.g. Jev pointwise 0.7552 → 0.7496). Latency variance across runs was not measured.
  • Long-context mode. Passage text exceeded 32,000 bytes for 92% (MIRACL) and 97% (JQaRA) of queries. OpenRouter’s Jev guide says such requests run in a slower, less accurate long-context mode (vendor claim, unverified); if true, Jev’s scores are conservative.
  • One prompt per mode, no tuning; no threshold or instruction search for any system.
  • “Pointwise” is shared-context (all passages in one state); per-pair requests were not compared. The ordinal Score primitive was not tried.
  • Cohere Rerank 5, named in Cohere’s documentation, was not in the OpenRouter catalog I used.
  • Costs and latencies are via OpenRouter, not vendor endpoints; results come from a single day.

Negative and unexpected results

  • Rank fusion does not help. Reciprocal rank fusion of two systems was significantly worse than the better member on JQaRA in all six pairs tried (e.g. Jev pointwise + Cohere 4-pro: −0.015 [−0.019, −0.010]) and indistinguishable from it on MIRACL. A hybrid pipeline is not supported by this data.
  • Refusals. For 5 of 1,667 JQaRA queries, gpt-6-luna in listwise mode returned “OpenAI refused to answer” on every retry; those queries were scored on the presented order. Cohere returned a score for every query.
  • Exclusions. Voyage rerank models returned HTTP 429 at sustained volume (2,237 times for the lite model over 1,667 queries) and hosted Qwen3-Reranker was slow or unavailable; their quality is not in the final table.
  • Qrels are incomplete. On MIRACL, 47 of 53 candidates that Jev scored ≥0.5 but the qrels called non-relevant were judged relevant by an independent LLM judge (phase-1 data, Jev-only) — absolute scores understate recall of true relevance.

Conclusion

Conclusion: what I learned

  • Rankings do not transfer between datasets. The same Jev 1.14 loses to Cohere 4-pro on MIRACL (0.750 vs 0.793) and wins on JQaRA (0.885 vs 0.819).
  • A decision model is a score-and-select API, not a drop-in cross-encoder. Listwise piles probability on ~6 of 100 passages (great top-1, shallow ranking); pointwise scores everything but ties a lot; P(yes) is a ranking score, not the probability of relevance.
  • Price structure matters more than list price. Tokens × price versus a flat per-query fee: Jev sits on the cost frontier on both datasets, Cohere 4-pro and gpt-6-luna never do.
  • Measure it yourself, and measure carefully. Client-side queueing had inflated my first latency numbers, rate limits removed Voyage, and one run per system is a real limit. Rank fusion of two systems did not help.
  • Practical rule: A/B on 50–200 of your own queries, with cost and latency in the table.
